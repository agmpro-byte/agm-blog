The AI Overview Takeover: Why the Old Playbook Is Broken

AI overviews are rewriting how homeowners find contractors. Here's what changed, why old SEO tricks fail, and what to do instead.

Jul 29, 2026 7 min read

A homeowner in your service area wakes up to a wasp nest under the eaves. Two years ago, she typed "wasp removal near me," scrolled past a couple of ads, clicked the top three results, and one of those clicks might have been you. Today she types the same thing and Google hands her a written answer at the top of the page — what to do, what it costs in general terms, when to call a pro, and a short list of names it decided are worth showing. She may never scroll to the blue links you spent years trying to rank.

That box at the top is an AI overview. Google builds it by reading across dozens of pages, stitching together an answer, and presenting it as the first and often only thing a searcher sees. For local home-services businesses — turf care, pest control, HVAC, plumbing — this is the biggest shift in how customers find you since the smartphone. And most of the advice you've been given about "doing SEO" was written for a world that no longer exists.

Let me walk you through what actually changed, why the old moves stopped working, and what a contractor can do about it without hiring an agency or pretending to be a software company.

What an AI overview actually is

Start with plain language, because the jargon around this gets thick fast.

Search used to work like a library card catalog. You asked a question, Google handed you a ranked list of pages, and you picked one. Your job as a business was to get your page as high on that list as possible. That's what "ranking" meant.

An AI overview changes the deal. Instead of just handing over a list, Google now reads the top pages for the searcher and writes a summary answer in its own words. The homeowner reads that summary and, more and more, stops there. The industry has a blunt name for this: the "zero-click" search — a search that gets answered without anyone clicking through to a website.

For a contractor, the practical effect is simple and a little uncomfortable. The traffic you used to earn by ranking on page one is being intercepted before it reaches you. Even when you do rank well, Google may quote a sentence from your page inside its own answer, satisfy the searcher, and never send that person to your site to see your phone number.

This isn't a temporary glitch. It's the direction Google has committed to. So the question stops being "how do I rank higher" and becomes "how do I get mentioned, cited, and chosen inside the answer itself."

Why the old playbook is broken

The SEO playbook most contractors were sold looks something like this: stuff your city name into your page titles, publish a "10 tips" blog post every month, pile up as many keywords as you can, and chase backlinks. Some of that was always thin advice. AI overviews turn the thin parts into dead weight. Here's why.

Keyword stuffing stopped mattering years ago — now it actively hurts. The whole point of an AI overview is that Google understands what a homeowner means, not just the exact words they typed. A page written for a robot — "Phoenix AC repair Phoenix air conditioning repair Phoenix" — reads as low quality to a system that's now judging whether your content is worth quoting. You're optimizing for a scoring method that was retired.

Generic "tips" content is exactly what the AI already writes itself. If your blog post says the same five obvious things every other pest control site says, the overview will summarize those points without you. You've handed Google the raw material to replace you. Content that gets cited is specific, local, and demonstrates real experience — the stuff a language model can't generate on its own because it's genuinely yours.

Ranking number one is no longer the finish line. You can be the top organic result and still lose the customer to an answer box sitting above you. The metric contractors were trained to celebrate — position one — has quietly become a consolation prize.

Volume was never the strategy, and now it's a liability. Publishing forty shallow pages to "cover more keywords" gives Google forty weak signals about your business. A handful of pages that clearly prove you know your trade and your town beats a content mill every time.

The uncomfortable truth: a lot of contractors are still paying for services built entirely around the broken version. The reports look busy. The rankings might even tick up. But the phone doesn't ring more, because the game moved.

What actually wins in the AI-overview era

Here's the part that should feel encouraging, because it plays to a real contractor's strengths rather than an agency's tricks.

Prove real experience, because that's what the AI can't fake. Google's systems increasingly favor content that shows first-hand expertise — actual jobs, actual local conditions, actual outcomes. A plumber writing about how hard water in a specific set of neighborhoods wrecks water heaters faster than the manufacturer's estimate is offering something no general answer-writer has. That specificity is what earns a citation inside the overview.

Answer the real question directly. AI overviews are assembled from pages that clearly and quickly answer what was asked. If a homeowner is wondering "why is my lawn brown in patches in July," the page that leads with a straight, correct answer — before any sales pitch — is the page Google trusts to quote. Bury the answer under three paragraphs of company history and you get skipped.

Get your business facts consistent everywhere. Your name, address, phone number, service area, and hours need to match across your website, your Google Business Profile, and every directory you're listed in. When those facts conflict, Google loses confidence in you, and a system trying to decide who to recommend will pass on a business it isn't sure about. This is unglamorous cleanup work, and it matters more than any clever headline.

Feed the profile Google shows in the box. For local searches, the overview often leans on your Google Business Profile — reviews, photos, recent activity, questions answered. A profile that's alive and accurate is doing quiet work that your website can't do alone.

Earn reviews that mention specifics. "Great service" helps a little. "They diagnosed a refrigerant leak our last company missed and had cool air back by afternoon" helps a lot, because it's specific, credible, and reinforces exactly the expertise the AI is trying to verify.

Notice what none of this requires: no keyword games, no monthly content mill, no pretending to understand algorithms. It requires that you be genuinely good at your trade and that the record of that skill be clean, consistent, and easy for a machine to read.

The harder problem underneath the SEO problem

Here's what most articles about AI overviews leave out. Getting found is only half the battle. The other half is what happens in the sixty seconds after a homeowner decides you might be the one.

When search sends fewer but higher-intent people your way — someone who read the answer, decided they need a pro, and is now choosing between two or three names — every one of those contacts is worth more than it used to be. Miss the call, take six hours to reply to the form, forget to follow up, and you've wasted the exact lead that AI-era search worked hard to hand you. The old world of cheap, abundant clicks forgave sloppy follow-up. This one doesn't.

This is where a lot of contractors realize the problem isn't really their website. It's that they can't see, in one place, where jobs are coming from, which leads went cold, and what's actually driving revenue versus what just looks busy.

Where to start

You don't need to overhaul everything this week. The shift to AI overviews is real, but it rewards fundamentals over gimmicks, and fundamentals are something a working contractor can control.

Clean up your business facts. Keep your Google profile alive. Write a few genuinely useful, genuinely local pages that prove you know your trade. Ask happy customers for specific reviews. And make sure that when a hard-won lead finally reaches you, you can actually see it and respond before your competitor does.

The old playbook told you to chase rankings. The new reality asks a better question: when the answer box sends someone your way, are you set up to catch them and close them?
