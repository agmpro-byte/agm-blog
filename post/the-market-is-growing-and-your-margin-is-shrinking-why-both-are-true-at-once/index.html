The Market Is Growing and Your Margin Is Shrinking: Why Both Are True at Once

Home services demand is up, margins are down, and bundled service contracts are taking share. Here's what that shift means for your shop.

Aug 7, 2026 8 min read

There is a strange thing happening in home services right now, and most owners feel it before they can name it.

The phone rings more than it did three years ago. Job counts are up. The pipeline looks healthy on paper. And yet the money left at the end of the month is thinner. You are busier and poorer at the same time, and nobody at the supply house has a clean explanation for why.

Both things are real. Demand for residential service work — lawn care and turf, pest control, HVAC, plumbing, electrical — has been expanding steadily across nearly every developed market, and it is expanding faster in places where housing stock is aging and homeowners have less time and less appetite for doing the work themselves. That is a genuine tailwind. It is also not the same thing as a profit tailwind.

What is squeezing you is happening on a different axis than what is growing.

Where the Growth Is Actually Coming From

Three forces are pushing residential service demand up, and none of them are about you being better at your job.

Housing stock is getting older. A large share of homes in North America, the UK, and Western Europe were built decades ago. Old houses need more work. Systems fail, sewer lines crack, insulation fails, roofs give up. The service work embedded in an aging housing stock does not go away in a soft economy — it just gets deferred and comes back angrier.

Homeowners are outsourcing more. Two-income households with long commutes do not spend Saturday spreading fertilizer. The share of homeowners who hire out work they used to do themselves has been rising for a long time, and it accelerated after 2020 when a lot of people discovered they hated yard work.

Weather and pests are less predictable. Longer growing seasons, expanded pest ranges, more heat load on cooling equipment, more freeze events in places that never used to freeze. Every one of those is a service call. The pattern is messier than it was, which means the demand is messier too — big spikes, longer shoulder seasons, weirder timing.

So the market grows. Good. Now here is the other half.

Where the Margin Is Going

Your cost base has moved faster than your price base, and it moved in ways that are hard to claw back.

Labor is the big one. Skilled trade labor is scarce almost everywhere, and it has been scarce long enough that it is a structural condition, not a cycle. You are paying more for techs than you were, you are paying more to keep them, and you are spending real money on the ones who leave in the first ninety days. Every hour a good tech spends driving, waiting, or redoing something is an hour you paid premium wages for and did not bill.

Acquisition cost is climbing. The cost of getting a new customer in front of you — paid search, lead marketplaces, aggregator platforms, review-site placement — has gone up in nearly every service category. Worse, a lot of those channels sell the same homeowner to three of your competitors at once. You pay for the lead, you drive to the estimate, you lose on price, and the cost stays on your books.

Equipment, parts, and fuel are not going back. Some of the spikes have eased. The baseline has not. Truck payments, refrigerant, chemicals, and replacement parts all cost more than they did in the last stable period you can remember.

Price sensitivity got sharper. Homeowners have gotten very good at comparison shopping. They can pull three quotes on their phone while standing in the driveway. That does not mean they always pick the cheapest — but it does mean an undifferentiated bid gets treated like a commodity, and commodities do not carry margin.

Put those together and you get the sensation most owners describe: growth without gain. More revenue passing through a leakier bucket.

The Bundled Contract Shift

Here is the part of the story that matters most, because it is where the winners are pulling away.

Across every major home-services category, share is quietly moving toward companies that sell an ongoing relationship instead of a series of transactions. Recurring maintenance agreements. Seasonal programs. Multi-service bundles that put turf, pest, and irrigation under one plan, or that pair HVAC maintenance with plumbing inspections and priority scheduling.

The big regional and national players have been leaning into this hard for years. Private-equity-backed roll-ups almost always do the same thing after an acquisition: convert one-off customers into contract customers as fast as possible. They do it because the math is better on almost every dimension that matters.

Predictable revenue changes how you run. When a meaningful share of next quarter is already committed, you can schedule crews tighter, buy materials smarter, and stop panic-discounting in the slow weeks. Route density improves because you know where you are going before the phone rings.

Acquisition cost gets amortized. If it costs you a certain amount to win a homeowner, that cost is brutal against a single job and reasonable against four years of service. Contract customers make your marketing spend defensible.

Attach rates go up. A homeowner already paying you monthly is dramatically easier to sell a second service to than a stranger. The bundle is not just a retention tool — it is the cheapest lead source you will ever have.

Price competition softens. Nobody comparison-shops a plan the way they comparison-shop a quote. Once you are the company that handles the yard and the bugs and the spring system check, you are not one of three bids anymore. You are the incumbent, and incumbents get called first.

Enterprise value goes up. If you ever want to sell the business or bring in a partner, buyers pay materially more for contracted recurring revenue than for a good reputation and a busy summer. This is the single most under-appreciated fact in the trades.

None of this is new information to the large operators. It is new pressure on the independent, because when the regional player down the road converts their base to contracts, they stop competing with you on individual jobs and start competing with you on owning the customer. That is a much harder fight to win from behind.

Why Bundles Are Hard for Small Shops — And What Actually Blocks Them

Most owners already know contracts are better. That is not the gap. The gap is operational.

A one-off job model is forgiving. Somebody calls, you go, you bill, it's done. Bundled contracts are unforgiving, because you have made a promise about the future and now you have to keep it without anybody reminding you.

The failure points are boring and consistent:

  • Renewals lapse silently. Nobody flags the customer whose plan ended in March until they call a competitor in June.
  • Scheduled visits get skipped. The second and third visits in a seasonal program are the ones that fall off when the crew is slammed. Then you get the churn.
  • Attach opportunities die in someone's head. The tech notices a failing water heater on a pest visit and mentions it in the truck. Nobody writes it down. Nothing happens.
  • Nobody knows which plans actually make money. Owners often discover, years in, that one of their bundles is a loss leader they never intended.
  • Billing friction eats retention. A declined card that nobody chases becomes a cancelled customer for reasons that have nothing to do with your work quality.

Every one of those is an information problem, not a work-ethic problem. The service gets delivered fine. What breaks is the tracking, the follow-through, and the visibility — the knowing what is happening across a couple hundred ongoing commitments while you are also running crews and answering the phone.

That is exactly the wall independents hit when they try to move to a contract model. They can sell the plans. They cannot see the plans.

What Visibility Actually Looks Like

Before you can defend a margin, you have to be able to see it. Most contractors moving into bundled work need three things they usually do not have:

A live picture of recurring revenue. How many active agreements, what they are worth, which ones renew in the next sixty days, and which ones are trending toward churn. Not a spreadsheet someone updates when they remember. A dashboard you can open on Monday morning and trust.

Job-level and plan-level profitability. Not just what the plan bills, but what it costs to deliver — labor hours, drive time, materials, callbacks. This is where owners find their surprises, and finding them early is worth more than almost anything else you could measure.

Follow-through that does not depend on memory. Renewal windows, missed visits, upsell notes from the field, failed payments. These need to surface as decisions in front of a person, not sit buried until they become problems.

What to Do With This

The market is going to keep growing. That is the easy part. The question is whether the growth lands in your business as revenue you keep or revenue you route straight to labor, ads, and fuel.

Three things worth doing this quarter, in order:

  1. Count your recurring revenue honestly. Not "most of our customers come back." Actual contracted, committed dollars for the next twelve months. Most owners are shocked by how small the real number is.
  2. Find your worst bundle. One of your plans is underwater. Find it before it scales.
  3. Fix one follow-through leak. Pick renewals or pick failed payments. Just one. The compounding on a fixed leak is better than the compounding on a new campaign.

You do not need to become a roll-up to compete with one. You need to be able to see your own business as clearly as they see theirs.
