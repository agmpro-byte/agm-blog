The Agentic Shift Skipped the Trades. So We Built It.

Enterprise software is racing toward AI that does the work, not just records it. Nobody built that for contractors — until now. What the agentic shift means for home service businesses.

Jul 21, 2026 6 min read

In March, Shelly Palmer — one of the most-read technology analysts in the enterprise world — published a piece called "Corporate Claws" telling Fortune 500 leaders they need a strategy for a new kind of AI: not chatbots, but persistent digital workers with memory, schedules, and the authority to get things done.

At NVIDIA's GTC conference, CEO Jensen Huang put it even more bluntly: "every single enterprise company … needs an agentic strategy."

Salesforce is building it. ServiceNow is building it. NVIDIA, OpenAI, and Anthropic are building the plumbing underneath it. The largest software companies on earth are all telling their largest customers the same thing: software that just records your work is over. Software that does your work is next.

Here's what none of them are saying: nobody sent that memo to the trades.

So we built it ourselves. This post walks through what the enterprise world is predicting, translates it out of boardroom-speak, and shows you the version that already exists for home service businesses — because the most useful thing about Palmer's article isn't the prediction. It's that for contractors, it isn't a prediction anymore.

What is a "claw," in plain English?

Palmer borrows the term for a specific kind of AI system — one that keeps working when you're not looking at it. Strip the jargon and a "claw" is four things:

  • It remembers. It carries context from yesterday into today, instead of starting blank every conversation.
  • It's connected. It can read your calendar, your inbox, your customer records — the systems where your business actually lives.
  • It runs on a schedule. It doesn't wait to be asked. It checks the pipeline Monday morning whether or not you do.
  • It can delegate. Big tasks get split into smaller ones and handed to helpers, the way a foreman runs a crew.

Palmer describes a three-tier stack underneath this: raw tools at the bottom (the AI models, the connections into other software), repeatable skills in the middle (encoded ways of doing a specific piece of work — writing a proposal, analyzing the numbers), and the persistent assistant on top that uses both and answers to a person.

If that structure sounds abstract, translate it into a business you already understand. The tools are your trucks and equipment. The skills are your standard operating procedures — the way your best crew lead does a walkthrough, every time, in the same order. The layer on top is the operations manager who knows which procedure to run, when, and who checks the result before it goes to the customer.

Enterprise software companies are spending billions to build that operations manager out of software. That's the whole story.

Why this matters for a home service business

Here's the question that should interest you more than anything happening at a Fortune 500 company: who is building this for a 12-person plumbing company? A turf installer? A pest control operator running three trucks?

The honest answer: almost nobody. Salesforce is selling Agentforce upmarket. ServiceNow sells to IT departments. The entire agentic wave is being designed for companies with a CIO — a chief information officer, the executive whose whole job is buying and managing software. You don't have one. You are one, at about 9:30 at night, after the quotes go out.

That gap is the reason we exist. As the Why Now section on our homepage puts it: the agentic shift is already here — it just skipped the trades. AGM Pro Tools is the agentic strategy for home service businesses, already running. Not a chatbot bolted onto your CRM. A working intelligence layer that reads your operational data, finds the revenue leaking through the gaps, and executes the fixes — with a human decision-maker always in the loop.

And the architecture matches the one Palmer describes, tier for tier, because we arrived at the same structure by building it — deterministic tools at the bottom, written operating procedures in the middle, and an accountable decision layer on top that a human owner directs. Enterprise is drawing the blueprint on a whiteboard. Contractors can walk through the finished building.

Prediction 1: Software stops recording work and starts doing it

Palmer's core claim is that the value of software shifts from storing information to completing workflows. Your field service platform — Jobber, FieldRoutes, Salesforce, whichever one runs your day — is a system of record. It knows a quote went out eleven days ago and nobody followed up. It has known that for eleven days. It just never did anything about it.

The agentic version doesn't wait. It notices the stalled quote, drafts the follow-up in your voice, and either sends it under rules you approved or puts it in front of you for a one-tap yes. Same data, opposite posture.

This is exactly the line AGM Pro Tools already draws: your software runs the job — AGM Pro Tools manages the growth. The follow-ups, the nurture campaigns, the review requests, the pipeline management that happens before the work and after it. You don't log into anything new and you don't replace anything. The intelligence layer sits on top of the platform you already run and works the part of the business that a system of record was never going to work.

Prediction 2: The economics of software flip

The second half of Palmer's argument is about money, and it's worth understanding even if you never read another analyst piece.

Enterprise software is priced per seat — per human logging in. Palmer's point (he predicts every software company becomes a "GaaS" company — agents-as-a-service): once software does the work instead of the human, charging per human stops making sense. You'd be paying for chairs nobody sits in. Software companies will be forced to price on work completed and outcomes delivered, and most of them will fight it, because it breaks the business model they went public on.

For a contractor, this flip is pure upside — if you're positioned for it. You shouldn't pay for logins. You should pay for recovered revenue: the stalled quote that closed, the past customer who rebooked, the review that got requested while the crew was still packing up.

That's the model we run today, not next year. Enterprise companies will get outcome-priced agentic software eventually, with an enterprise price tag and an 18-month rollout. AGM Pro Tools can deliver it this quarter.

Prediction 3: Autonomy without oversight is the new risk

Palmer spends a serious stretch of the article warning enterprise security chiefs about what happens when autonomous software gets access to email, files, and calendars with nobody watching. He's right to. An AI that can do things is a fundamentally different responsibility than an AI that can only say things, and the enterprise world is still arguing about how to govern it.

You don't need a security department to act on the contractor version of that lesson. It's one question, asked of any vendor who wants to automate part of your business: who approves the action before it happens?

Our answer has been the same since day one, and it's on the homepage in plain sight: a human decision-maker, always in the loop. The system reads, analyzes, drafts, and recommends around the clock — but the moves that matter route through an owner's judgment. Autonomy where it's safe, approval where it counts. If a vendor can't give you a clear answer to that question, keep your hand on your wallet.

What the enterprise wave validates — and what to do about it

When Salesforce, ServiceNow, and NVIDIA all point the same direction, the debate about whether is over. The only open questions are when it reaches your industry and who builds it for businesses your size. Palmer's article is third-party confirmation of the category. The trades just weren't in the room when it was announced.

Three things worth doing this week:

  1. Ask your current software what it does while you sleep. Not what it stores — what it does. If the answer is "sends you a notification," you have a system of record, not an agentic strategy.
  2. Inventory your leaks before you buy anything. Unworked quotes, past customers nobody re-contacts, reviews never requested. Agentic software is only as valuable as the revenue it recovers, so know where yours is leaking first.
